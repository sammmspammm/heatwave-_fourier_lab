How hot will the afternoon get?

The temperature through a day rises and falls like a wave. A Fourier series breaks that wave into a few simple, smooth waves. With them we can find the hottest hour, see how long the heat stays dangerous, and decide whether to warn people.

1The problem

We have the temperature for every hour of one day, from 00:00 to 24:00. We want to answer three simple questions:

  • How hot does the afternoon get, and at what time?
  • For how many hours is it dangerously hot?
  • Does this day count as a heatwave?

A list of 25 raw readings is noisy and hard to read. A Fourier series turns it into one smooth curve described by only a handful of numbers.

Fourier series

Any repeating curve can be written as a steady level plus waves that go up and down at different speeds.

Half-range series

We only have one day of data, so we make a repeating curve by adding a mirror copy. A plain mirror gives a cosine series. A flipped mirror gives a sine series.

Parseval's identity

The energy of the day equals the energy of all its waves added together. So we can measure how much each wave matters and how much is still missing.

The mirror trick, using the day shown above

Plain mirror, cosine series

The curve meets itself smoothly at midnight. This is why the cosine series fits well.

Flipped mirror, sine series

The flipped copy makes a big jump at midnight. The sine series has to work hard to fit that jump.

2The data

Choose where the hourly temperatures come from. Everything on this page updates to match.

This day is made by a formula so you can change it. It imitates a hot pre-monsoon day in central India with 25 hourly readings (00:00 to 24:00).

temperature = average level + swing × daily wave (hottest at 15:00) + 0.18 × swing × smaller wave (sharpens the afternoon peak) + sensor noise

Why these numbers: an average near 35 °C with a 7.5 °C swing gives an afternoon peak near 43 °C, which is typical of a plains heatwave day. The noise imitates a real sensor.

Accepted: 24 or 25 numbers for one day, a multiple of 24 for several days, or CSV rows with a date-time and a temperature. The Open-Meteo hourly CSV works as it is.

Where to find real readings: Open-Meteo Historical Weather (choose hourly temperature_2m and export CSV), data.gov.in, India Meteorological Department station records, or your own sensor log. Check each source's terms and cite it in your submission.

3Split the day into waves

The cosine series writes the day as a steady level plus waves. Each wave is a cosine that completes n half-turns across the 24 hours.

f(t) ≈ a₀/2 + a₁ cos(πt/24) + a₂ cos(2πt/24) + a₃ cos(3πt/24) + … aₙ = (2/24) ∫₀²⁴ f(t) cos(nπt/24) dt

In words: a₀/2 is the average temperature of the day. Each aₙ says how strong wave number n is. The integral compares the day with the wave: the more they look alike, the bigger aₙ. Wave n repeats every 48/n hours, so n = 2 is the 24-hour daily cycle and n = 4 is the 12-hour cycle.

5One full number example

Here is how one wave strength is computed from the readings, using the cosine series. The integral is replaced by a sum over the hourly readings (the trapezoid rule, where the first and last readings count half).

A classroom example you can do by hand
f(x) = x on (0, π), cosine series a₀ = (2/π)∫₀^π x dx = π aₙ = (2/π)∫₀^π x cos nx dx = 2((−1)ⁿ − 1)/(π n²) = −4/(π n²) for odd n, 0 for even n x ≈ π/2 − (4/π)(cos x + cos 3x/9 + cos 5x/25 + …)
Parseval check (1/π)∫₀^π x² dx = π²/3 a₀²/4 + ½ Σ aₙ² = π²/4 + (8/π²) Σ 1/n⁴ (n odd) π²/3 − π²/4 = π²/12 = (8/π²) Σ 1/n⁴ so 1 + 1/3⁴ + 1/5⁴ + … = π⁴/96 ≈ 1.01468

6Energy check with Parseval

Parseval's identity says the energy of the day equals the energy of its waves added together:

cosine: (1/24) ∫₀²⁴ f(t)² dt = a₀²/4 + ½ (a₁² + a₂² + a₃² + …) sine: (1/24) ∫₀²⁴ f(t)² dt = ½ (b₁² + b₂² + b₃² + …)

In words: the left side is the total energy E of the day. Each wave adds a small share of it. The share that is still missing after N waves is the error, so the average error is √(E − energy kept).

Most of the energy sits in the average level, so this number is always very close to 100 %. The next bar removes the average and shows how well the waves explain the daily ups and downs, which is what matters for the peak.

      Limits of this analysis
      
  
        
    
      • The example day is made by a formula, not measured. Real days also have clouds, wind and humidity changes.
        • 
    
      • Hourly readings can only show waves that repeat every 2 hours or slower, so the series stops at 24 waves and this page stops at 20.
        • 
    
      • The mirror copy is a maths trick. Real temperature does not mirror itself at midnight.
        • 
    
      • The integrals are done with the trapezoid rule on 25 readings, so Parseval's identity holds only approximately.
        • 
    
      • The warning levels are simplified. A real warning also uses departure from normal, humidity and how many days in a row it is hot.
        • 
    
      • One station and one day cannot show whether a heatwave will last.
        • 
  
      

      


      
  
      Conclusion
      
  
      A day of temperature readings can be written as a steady level plus a few smooth waves. The cosine series fits better than the sine series because its mirror copy has no jump at midnight. Parseval's identity turns the wave sizes into an energy budget, so we know how much of the day each wave explains and how much error is left. A handful of waves is enough to find the afternoon peak and the hot window and to give a clear heatwave warning with a stated confidence.
